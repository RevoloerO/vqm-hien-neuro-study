1. Neuro Foundations: Cranial Nerves & IICP

Cranial Nerves (12)

  • IICP causes vital sign changes from pressure on the thalamus / hypothalamus (body temperature) and the pons & medulla (BP, HR, RR)

I – Olfactory — Sense of smell; Test: eyes closed, one nostril closed, gently inhale coffee or soap; repeat other side

II – Optic — Controls central and peripheral vision; Test: central → count fingers 6 in. away; peripheral → confrontation test; IICP: blurred vision, diplopia, changes in movements

III – Oculomotor — Pupillary constriction; Test: dim lights, penlight from the periphery, check constriction; follow finger through 6 cardinal fields of gaze; IICP: ipsilateral dilated pupil, sluggish/no light response, can't look up, ptosis

IV – Trochlear — Moves eye down and toward the nose; Test: follow finger toward the nose

V – Trigeminal — 1) Face sensation (forehead, cheek, jaw) 2) Temporal & masseter muscles; Test: soft and dull object on all 3 areas; open mouth → jaw strength

VI – Abducens — Eye movement side to side; Test: look toward each ear; 6 cardinal fields; watch for nystagmus

VII – Facial — Facial movement and expression; Test: wrinkle forehead, close eyes, smile, pucker, show teeth, puff cheeks → check symmetry

VIII – Vestibulocochlear — Hearing (+ balance [Added]); Test: whispered voice test

IX – Glossopharyngeal — Tongue and throat: taste on the back of the tongue, swallow; Test: gag reflex, swallow, taste on back of tongue

X – Vagus — Tongue and throat: palate & uvula; Test: gag reflex; say "AHHH" → uvula midline, palate rises; [Added] IX vs X: both check gag. IX = taste on the back of the tongue. X = uvula/palate (and parasympathetic supply to the heart and gut)

XI – Spinal accessory — Neck and shoulder movement; Test: shrug shoulders; turn head side to side

XII – Hypoglossal — Tongue; Test: stick out tongue → should be midline

Intracranial Pressure Basics

Monro-Kellie Doctrine

  • If one component increases, another must decrease to maintain ICP
  • 3 components (volume can change in any): brain tissue, blood, CSF

Cerebral Blood Flow & CPP

  • Cerebral blood flow = main mechanism for autoregulation of ICP to maintain CPP; ⇒ CPP = MAP − ICP
  • Brain uses ~20% of body O₂ and ~25% of body glucose
  • Normal ICP responds to: arterial pressure, venous pressure, intra-abdominal & intrathoracic pressure, posture, temperature, blood gases (CO₂)

Stages of Increased ICP (4)

  • Normal ICP = 5–15 mm Hg · Elevated if >20 mm Hg sustained

Stage 1 — Total compensation

Stage 2 — ↓ compensation; risk for ↑ICP

Stage 3 — Failing compensation; clinical signs of ↑ICP (Cushing's triad)

Stage 4 — Herniation imminent → death

Cushing's Triad = NEUROLOGIC EMERGENCY (a LATE sign)

  1. Systolic hypertension with widening pulse pressure
  2. Bradycardia with bounding pulse
  3. Irregular, decreased respirations

Causes of ↑ICP

Factors affecting blood flow (5) — Mass lesion · Head injury (surgery) · Infections · Vascular insult · Toxic or metabolic encephalopathic conditions

Types of cerebral edema (3) — Vasogenic · Cytotoxic · Interstitial

Brain Injury

  • Primary: happens at the time of injury (car accident impact, blunt force trauma)
  • Secondary: develops hours to days after: hypoxia, ischemia, hypotension, edema, ↑ICP
  • Head injury terms: skull fracture · diffuse injury = concussion, diffuse axonal injury · focal injury = contusion
  • Complications: epidural, subdural, intracerebral hematoma

Assessment: Signs & Symptoms of ↑ICP

  • Change in LOC = FIRST sign! (Glasgow Coma Scale)
  • Change in vital signs (Cushing's) = LATE sign
  • ABCs · Headache · Vomiting · Decreased motor function (strength, response, hemiplegia)
  • Posturing: decerebrate, decorticate · Abnormal respiratory patterns
  • Ocular signs: dilated, unilateral, fixed pupil
  • Complications: inadequate perfusion, herniation

Diagnostics

  • CT · MRI · EEG · ICP measurement · Cerebral O₂ monitor (LICOX, Neurovent, jugular venous bulb) · Doppler US (transcranial Doppler = noninvasive, tracks cerebrovascular resistance)
  • NO lumbar puncture → contraindicated [Added] a sudden pressure drop can cause herniation

Nursing Interventions: Optimize ICP

  • Respiratory function = PRIORITY (maintain ventilator support)
  • Keep temp 36–37 °C (96.8–98.6 °F)
  • HOB 30° (not higher or lower) · head neutral · no extreme neck flexion · turn slowly · avoid coughing, straining, Valsalva, hip flexion
  • Pain & anxiety management · fluid/electrolyte balance · minimize abdominal distention (interferes with breathing; may need NG)
  • Quiet environment; don't do everything at once (space out care)
  • Prevent immobility complications · protect from self-injury · psychological care · sedation

ICP Monitoring: Ventriculostomy

  • Prevent and monitor for infection · measure as mean pressure · record waveform (normal, elevated, plateau waves)

Causes of inaccurate readings (5) — CSF leaks · obstruction/kinks in catheter · difference in height of bolt/transducer · incorrect height of drainage system · bubbles/air in tubing

Collaborative Care

  • Treat the underlying cause · Oxygenation: PaO₂ > 100 mm Hg, PaCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg
  • Nutrition: hypermetabolic/hypercatabolic → ↑glucose need; enteral or parenteral; early feeding within 3 days
  • Keep normovolemic: 0.9% NaCl preferred over D5W or 0.45% NaCl
  • Avoid hypoxia, hypoglycemia, acidosis → helps control ICP

Medications

Mannitol (Osmitrol) — Plasma expansion + osmotic effect → monitor fluid & electrolytes

Hypertonic saline — Moves water out of cells into blood → monitor BP and serum Na⁺

Corticosteroids — For vasogenic edema

Meds for complications / risks of ↑ICP (5) — Antiseizure · antipyretics · sedatives · analgesics · barbiturates

  • Goals: patent airway · ICP within normal limits · normal fluid/electrolytes · no immobility complications. Outcomes: ICP & CPP normal; no spikes during care

2. Stroke

  • Stroke = ischemia or hemorrhage into the brain → death of brain cells. Also called brain attack or CVA

Priority? PREVENTION!

Modifiable risk factors (10) — HTN · heart disease · diabetes · smoking · obesity · sleep apnea · metabolic syndrome · lack of exercise · poor diet · drug & alcohol use

Non-modifiable risk factors (5) — Age · gender · ethnicity/race · family history · history of TIA

Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)

  • TIA = warning sign: a STROKE IS COMING!
  • Temporary blockage → ischemia without infarction; stroke symptoms that typically last < 1 hour
  • 1/3 progress to ischemic stroke
  • [Added] TIA vs stroke? At onset you can't tell them apart, so treat as a stroke. TIA = symptoms resolve fully and imaging shows no infarct

Types of Stroke

Ischemic: MOST COMMON — Thrombus (60%): narrowing/thrombosis of vessel; associated with HTN and DM; TIA often precedes; recurrence common; Embolism: mostly from the heart, esp. A-fib; can be fat or air; Extent depends on: rapidity of onset, lesion size, collateral circulation; ⇒ Prognosis depends on how much brain tissue lost its blood supply

Hemorrhagic: ~13% — Intracerebral: HTN = most common cause; occurs during activity; often starts with a severe headache + N/V; Subarachnoid (SAH): usually a ruptured cerebral aneurysm; cerebral vasospasm is a common complication (ICU 14 days to monitor)

Assessment: Signs & Symptoms

  • FAST (Cincinnati, prehospital): Face drooping · Arm weakness/drift · Speech slurred · Time to call 911
  • Watch for SUDDEN: numbness/weakness (one side) · confusion/trouble speaking · trouble seeing · trouble walking/dizziness · severe headache with no known cause
  • NIHSS = primary clinical tool for acute stroke; measures severity, predicts short- & long-term outcomes; scored initially to compare treatment effect / risk vs benefit

Symptoms by Artery (4)

Anterior cerebral — Contralateral motor/sensory, sucking/rooting reflex, rigidity, gait problems, loss of proprioception & fine touch

Middle cerebral — Dominant side → aphasia, motor & sensory, hemianopsia; Non-dominant → neglect, motor & sensory, hemianopsia

Posterior cerebral — Hemianopsia, motor, pain, visual hallucinations

Vertebral — Cranial nerve signs, diplopia, dizziness, N/V, dysarthria, dysphasia, coma

Nursing Assessment by Function

Motor: most obvious effect — Impaired mobility, respiration, swallowing/speech, gag reflex, self-care. Loss of skilled movement, akinesia, altered tone and reflexes; ⇒ Initial flaccidity (days to weeks) → then spasticity** (loss of upper motor neuron control) · hyporeflexia → hyperreflexia

Communication: aphasia types — Aphasia = dominant hemisphere damage (dysphasia used interchangeably); Receptive = loss of comprehension · Expressive = loss of language production · Global = total inability; Nonfluent (Broca's) = minimal, slow speech · Fluent (Wernicke's) = speech present but little meaning; Dysarthria = disturbed muscular control** of speech (pronunciation, articulation, phonation)

Affect — Exaggerated/unpredictable emotions; worsened by depression, body image change, loss of function

Intellectual function — Memory and judgment impaired; Left = cautious, Right = impulsive

Spatial-perceptual (more often a RIGHT-brain stroke) — Incorrect perception of self and illness · unilateral neglect** · agnosia · apraxia

Elimination — Problems mostly initial and temporary; one-hemisphere stroke → bladder prognosis excellent

  • Diagnostics: CT · CTA · MRI/MRA · angiography · ultrasound · LP-CSF · EKG · glucose · CBC · PT/PTT

Acute Care: Ischemic Stroke

  • Time of onset is the most important part of the history → stroke center → meet door-to-needle guidelines
  • Emergency: ABCs → Stroke Alert → IV/BP → CT to rule out hemorrhage → check blood glucose (treat hypoglycemia) → labs → thrombolytic → keep NPO
  • Monitor: neuro deficit (NIHSS), ↑ICP, fluids/electrolytes, glycemic control, Stroke Core Measures
  • Elevated BP is common right after a stroke [Added] why: compensation to keep perfusing the at-risk brain (penumbra)

BP numbers to memorize; • No tPA: lower BP only if > 220/120; • Giving tPA: get BP < 185/110 before, and keep SBP < 185 during monitoring; • Hemorrhagic: goal SBP < 160; • Prevention: SBP < 140

Fibrinolytic: tPA — IV: 3–4.5 hrs from onset · Intra-arterial: up to 6 hrs; Do NOT give if history of: GI bleed, coagulation disorder, head trauma, major surgery, internal bleeding; Monitor closely for 24 hrs · Aspirin 24–48 hrs after · anticoagulants once stabilized · statins · also endovascular therapy

Acute Care: Hemorrhagic Stroke

  • Main focus = hypertension management (oral & IV agents) → SBP < 160
  • Anticoagulants & platelet inhibitors are CONTRAINDICATED
  • Surgery: evacuate hematoma · clip or coil aneurysm · ventriculostomy · hyperdynamic therapy (hypertension, hypervolemia)
  • Calcium channel blockers → treat vasospasm · seizure prophylaxis case-by-case

Post-Acute Nursing by System

  • After 12–24 hrs stable, care shifts from preserving life → lessening disability (rehab unit, outpatient, home)

Respiratory = PRIORITY — Frequent assessment, O₂, oral care, suction · prevent aspiration → swallow eval to rule out dysphagia

Neuro — Ongoing NIHSS, LOC, ICP

Cardiovascular — Fluid balance (dehydration vs overload), vitals · VTE prophylaxis: keep moving, ROM, SCDs/TED hose, LMWH

Musculoskeletal: prevent contractures — Trochanter roll (prevents hip external rotation) · hand cones · posterior leg splints/footboards/high-tops (prevent foot drop) · slings/lap boards · never pull the patient by the arm (shoulder displacement)

Integumentary — Reposition, special mattress/cushions · on the weak side for 30 min max · hygiene, emollients, early mobility

Nutrition — NPO until swallow eval · IV fluids initially, may need enteral · supports for self-feeding

GI / Urinary — Constipation = most common bowel problem → stool softeners/fiber, activity · bladder retraining · avoid indwelling catheters

Communication with aphasia — Simple, short sentences · check understanding · yes/no questions · normal volume/tone · give time · decrease stimuli · use gestures

Sensory-perceptual — Break tasks into simple steps · nonslip socks · teach about one-side neglect (may shave half the face); Homonymous hemianopsia vs neglect: first place items/food in the visual field, then teach the patient to scan

Coping — For emotional outbursts: distract, explain to family, calm environment, never shame or scold · explain diagnosis and procedures · active listening · include family, family conferences, social services

Stroke Prevention

  • ↓ sodium · normal weight · low saturated fat · limit alcohol · SBP < 140 · exercise 150 min/week · no smoking · control DM · cardiac meds · antiplatelets; anticoagulants for A-fib · left atrial appendage occlusion · surgical prevention

3. Meningitis & Encephalitis

Bacterial Meningitis

  • Acute inflammation of the meninges around the brain & spinal cord · fall, winter, early spring
  • Risk: students in dorms, prisoners
  • MEDICAL EMERGENCY: untreated mortality near 100%

Key signs — Fever · severe headache · N/V · nuchal rigidity · rash/petechiae (meningococcus) · coma (5–10%, poor prognosis)

Other signs — Photophobia · ↓LOC · signs of ↑ICP · seizures in 1/3 · headache worsens progressively with vomiting and irritability

Acute Interventions

  • Cultures (nasopharynx + CSF) BEFORE antibiotics · LP to check CSF (normally done)
  • Start empiric antibiotics early (critical); stop if viral
  • Respiratory isolation until cultures negative · prophylactic antibiotics for close contacts [Added] why: to prevent spread/outbreak

  • Comfort: darkened room, cool cloth over eyes (photophobia) · position for comfort · minimize stimuli · avoid restraints · family at bedside

  • Prevention: vaccinate at age 11; if missed → college freshmen (dorms)

  • Home care: weeks of convalescence · gradual activity · nutrition, rest · ROM & warm baths for rigidity · monitor recovery of vision, hearing, cognition, motor/sensory · support caregivers
  • Goals/outcomes: max neuro function, resolve infection, control pain; oriented ×3, normal temp

Viral Meningitis

  • Treatment: it's a virus → manage symptoms
  • [Added] Bacterial vs viral (CSF): bacterial = cloudy, ↑protein, ↓glucose, neutrophils; viral = clear, normal glucose, lymphocytes
  • [Added] While waiting for results: treat as bacterial (isolation + empiric antibiotics)

Encephalitis

  • Acute inflammation of the BRAIN; serious, sometimes fatal; caused by viruses
  • Spread by ticks or mosquitoes (e.g., West Nile); some are regional/seasonal · CMV encephalitis common in AIDS
  • S/S: nonspecific onset: fever, headache, N/V; signs in 2–3 days; ranges from mild mental status change to coma; any CNS abnormality possible
  • Care: symptomatic & supportive (ICU initially) · antiseizure drugs (may be prophylactic) · prevention = mosquito control, repellents

4. Seizures

  • Seizure = sudden, abnormal, excessive electrical discharge of brain neurons
  • Epilepsy / seizure disorder = 2+ seizures > 24 hrs apart without an underlying problem; most are idiopathic
  • Causes: stroke, TBI, hyper/hypoglycemia, electrolyte imbalance, alcohol withdrawal, high fever in children
  • ⇒ If the seizure stops once the systemic/metabolic problem is corrected → NOT a seizure disorder
  • Manifestations depend on the site · 3 classes: generalized, focal (60%), unknown onset

Seizure Phases (4)

Prodromal — Precedes the seizure, with signs or activity

Aura — Sensory warning; part of the seizure

Ictal — From first symptom to end of seizure activity

Postictal — Rest and recovery

Seizure Types

Generalized: BOTH hemispheres — Impaired consciousness, seconds to minutes; Motor: tonic-clonic, tonic, clonic, atonic; Non-motor (absence):** typical, atypical

Focal: ONE hemisphere — Focal irritation in a specific cortex region; sensory, motor, cognitive or emotional; Focal aware: conscious & alert; odd feelings (sudden sob, anger, nausea), twitching, sees/tastes/feels things not real; Focal impaired awareness: eyes open but unaware; may do embarrassing things; lasts 1–2 min**, then resumes activity without remembering

Motor terms (5) — Atonic = loss of tone · Tonic = sustained stiffening · Clonic = rhythmic jerking · Myoclonic = very rapid, brief, irregular jerks · Automatisms = lip smacking, repetitive behaviors; Non-motor: emotional (sudden sadness, joy) or autonomic

  • Status epilepticus = continuous seizure activity, or seizures in rapid succession with no return of consciousness
  • Subclinical seizure = seizing but sedated, so it can't be seen clinically
  • Pseudoseizure (psychogenic) [Added] seizure-like events without abnormal brain electrical activity (normal EEG); treated with psychological support, not antiseizure drugs

Post-Seizure Assessment & Diagnosis

  • Bitten tongue, soft tissue injury, cyanosis · abnormal RR, apnea (ictal), airway occlusion · HTN, tachy/bradycardia · incontinence, salivation · weakness, paralysis, ataxia (postictal)
  • History: anything different? missed or late dose? injury? other illness (vomiting)?
  • Diagnostics (rule out cause): CBC · CMP · CT/MRI/PET · LP · UA · liver enzymes · EEG

Tonic-Clonic Emergency Care

  1. Ensure airway
  2. Protect from injury: NEVER restrain; pad rails; help to the floor if in a chair; protect head; loosen clothing
  3. Nothing in the mouth → turn on side
  4. Establish IV (or IO) · stay with patient, time the seizure
  5. Give benzodiazepines to stop it
  6. Prepare to suction, assist ventilation, intubate if needed
  • Most seizures are self-limiting. First seizure or injury → seek care. Main treatment = meds; others: surgery, VNS, responsive neurostimulation, ketogenic diet

Seizure Medications

Seizure type Drugs
Tonic-clonic & focal onset Phenytoin, Tegretol, Depakote
Generalized non-motor (absence) & myoclonic Zarontin, divalproex, Klonopin
Focal Lamictal, Keppra
Status epilepticus Short-acting benzos first (IM, buccal, intranasal, IV): IV Ativan preferred → then a long-acting drug
  • Assess side effects & toxicity · teach compliance · effects differ in older adults · goal: therapeutic drug levels

Education & Complications

  • Preventing recurrence = MAJOR goal → take meds as prescribed; don't adjust dose without the provider; keep appointments
  • Safety (helmet if needed) · identify & avoid triggers · avoid excess alcohol, fatigue, sleep loss · teach family emergency care · medical alert bracelet · driving restrictions · referrals
  • Complications: injury/death from trauma (loss of consciousness = highest risk) · mortality 2–3× the general population · lifestyle effect = most common complication (stigma, employment, driving)

5. Headaches

  • Primary (most common, functional): migraine, tension-type, cluster
  • Secondary: caused by another condition (sinus infection, neck injury, stroke)
  • Pain-sensitive structures: venous sinuses, dura, cranial blood vessels, CN V, VII, IX, X

Causes of secondary headache (8) — Subarachnoid hemorrhage · brain tumors · intracranial masses · vascular abnormalities · trigeminal neuralgia · eye/nose/teeth disease · systemic illness (bacteremia, CO poisoning, mountain sickness, polycythemia vera) · medication-overuse headache (acetaminophen, NSAIDs, aspirin, sumatriptan, opioids)

Tension-Type

  • Bilateral, pressing/tightening, constant dull pressure, frontal-occipital · mild–moderate · neck pain, ↑ neck muscle tone · intermittent for weeks to years · photophobia, phonophobia · NO N/V, NO prodrome

Migraine

  • Unilateral (mostly), throbbing, 4–72 hrs · triggers (foods, trauma, menstruation) · genetic link 50%
  • Without aura = most common · With aura = only 25%; aura 10–30 min before (visual, sensory, motor)
  • Photophobia, N/V, facial numbness/tingling · patients "hibernate" (escape light, noise, odors)

Cluster

  • Rare · sharp, stabbing, one of the most severe · daily for weeks–months, then remission · same time of day · trigeminal nerve · alcohol trigger
  • Unilateral, minutes to 3 hrs, around the eye → temple, cheek, nose, gums · eye edema, tearing, ptosis, constricted pupil, rhinitis, flushing · patient agitated & restless

Diagnosis, Nursing, Drugs

  • Diagnostics: neuro exam · CT only if a secondary cause is suspected · CBC, BMP, UA
  • Nursing: type of headache guides therapy · headache diary · exercise, relaxation, socializing · quiet dim room · massage/heat (tension) · written list of meds (prevents accidental overdose) · avoid triggers (food, smoke, perfume; triggers may change)

Tension drugs — Aspirin/NSAID ± caffeine, sedative, muscle relaxant · Prevent: tricyclic antidepressant, antiseizure, muscle relaxant

Migraine drugs — Mild–mod: NSAID, aspirin, caffeine analgesics · Mod–severe: TRIPTANS first line · triptans contraindicated in some patients (vasoconstriction) · preventive therapy

Cluster drugs — Triptans standard · 100% O₂ at 7–12 L/min for 20 min · preventive therapy

6. Parkinson's Disease

  • Disease of the basal ganglia: chronic, progressive, neurodegenerative, no acute exacerbations
  • Lack of dopamine → disrupts the dopamine–acetylcholine balance · Lewy bodies · cause unknown (environment + genes) · ↑ with age · more common in men

Classic Signs (TRAP)

T – Tremor — At rest; "pill rolling" (thumb and forefinger)

R – Rigidity — "Cogwheel" rigidity (intermittent catches)

A – Akinesia/Bradykinesia — Slow to start and carry out movement; loss of automatic movement

P – Postural instability — Can't stop; shuffling, propulsive gait, flexed arms (later stages)

  • Early: mild tremor, slight limp, ↓ arm swing · speech, sleep, eating problems
  • Non-motor: depression, anxiety, apathy, fatigue, pain, incontinence, constipation, ED, memory changes

Nursing

  • Diet: small pieces · 6 small meals · moderate fiber · limit protein to evening [Added] protein competes with levodopa absorption
  • Bradykinesia tips: rock side to side to start · imagine stepping over a line · lift toes · chairs with arms (blocks under back legs) · remove rugs · simple clothing · raised toilet seat · ottoman · sleep hygiene
  • Exercise, independence, prevent contractures & falls · caregiver stress (dementia)

Collaborative & Complications

  • No cure → symptom relief · PT, speech, nutrition · DBS, ablation surgery
  • Levodopa + Carbidopa (Sinemet) = primary treatment · 1 drug best, combination later · excess dopaminergic drugs → paradoxical intoxication
  • Complications ↑ with progression: dysphagia → aspiration/malnutrition · orthostatic hypotension · falls · dyskinesias · dementia in 70% · pneumonia, UTI, skin breakdown

7. Multiple Sclerosis & Myasthenia Gravis

Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

  • Chronic progressive CNS disorder: segmental demyelination of brain & spinal cord nerve fibers
  • Onset 20–40 yrs · women 2–3× more · temperate climates · cause unknown (infectious, immune, genetic)

3 pathologic processes — Chronic inflammation · demyelination · gliosis (scarring)

4 disease patterns — Relapsing-remitting (85%) · primary-progressive · secondary-progressive · progressive-relapsing

  • Vision changes = often FIRST sign · weakness, fatigue, tingling/numbness, spasms · Lhermitte's sign (electric shock down the spine) · pain · bowel/bladder · cognitive/emotional
  • Triggers to avoid: infection, trauma, live virus vaccines, childbirth, stress, climate
  • Nursing: balance exercise & rest · limit caffeine · high-fiber diet (constipation) · self-cath if needed · reassure during diagnosis · prevent immobility complications in exacerbation
  • No cure · disease-modifying: interferons, monoclonal antibodies, synthetics, immunomodulators (often first) · corticosteroids · symptom drugs: antidepressants/antiseizure (pain), anticholinergics (bladder) · PT (spasticity, coordination, water exercise)

Myasthenia Gravis (MG)

  • Autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction · fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness · may respond to thymectomy
  • Muscles used repeatedly: eyes/eyelids, chewing, swallowing, speaking, breathing, trunk, limbs
  • Strongest in the morning, exhausted with activity · no sensory loss, normal reflexes

Danger: weakness reaches swallowing & breathing → may need a ventilator; Myasthenic crisis = acute worsening of weakness; triggers: infection, surgery, stress, drugs, pregnancy, corticosteroids; Cholinergic crisis [Added] = too much anticholinesterase medication → weakness plus cholinergic signs (salivation, sweating, cramps, fasciculations); ⇒ Treatment: try to balance avoiding a myasthenic or cholinergic crisis

  • Nursing: usually hospitalized for respiratory infection or crisis · drug teaching & contraindications · recognize crisis · soft, easy-to-chew diet · Collaborative: thymectomy, anticholinesterase, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants

8. Delirium, Dementia & Alzheimer's

Dementia vs Delirium vs Depression

Feature Dementia Delirium Depression
Onset Subtle, gradual Abrupt Often abrupt, with life changes
Progression Slow Abrupt Variable
Duration Years Hours → weeks Months → years
Thinking Poor abstract thinking & judgment, word-finding Disorganized, distorted, incoherent speech Intact; apathy, indecisive, hopeless
Perception Misperceptions, delusions, hallucinations Distorted, delusions, hallucinations May deny; guilt
Psychomotor Pacing/hyperactive → limited Variable Withdrawn, hypoactive
Sleep Fragmented; up at night Reversed sleep-wake Early-morning waking

Delirium

Causes: the DELIRIUM mnemonic — Dementia, Dehydration · Electrolytes, Emotional stress · Lung, liver, heart, kidney, brain · Infection, ICU · Rx drugs · Injury, Immobility · Untreated pain, Unfamiliar environment · Metabolic

  • Risk: >65, male, cognitive, drugs, environment, immobility, medical, sensory, surgery
  • Diagnosis: history + Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) + labs to find the cause
  • Nursing: prevention, early recognition, treat the cause · near the nurses' station, sitter · reorient (clocks, calendars, familiar objects) · early mobility · avoid restraints · drugs only if needed

Alzheimer's Disease (AD)

  • NOT a normal part of aging · chronic, progressive, irreversible neurodegeneration
  • Brain: amyloid plaques · neurofibrillary tangles · lost neuron connections · neuron death · changes start ≥ 15 yrs before symptoms
  • Risk: age (greatest), family history, cardiovascular, head trauma
  • Diagnosis by exclusion: no definitive test except postmortem · CT/MRI/PET, biomarkers, neuropsych testing
Mild Moderate Severe
Forgetful beyond normal; short-term memory loss; loses initiative/interests; forgets recent events/names; impatient; can't do simple math; trouble planning Obvious memory loss & confusion; help dressing; incontinence; forgets family names; agitation, wandering, poor judgment; delusions, hallucinations All cognition severely impaired; no self-care; may not talk or understand words; eating/swallowing problems; immobile; incontinent

Behavioral Problems

  • Repetitiveness, delusions, hallucinations, agitation, aggression = the patient's response to a trigger (pain, frustration, temperature, anxiety)
  • Strategies: redirection · distraction · reassurance · never threaten or restrain
  • Sundowning = more confused/agitated in late afternoon/evening → assess cause · calm quiet environment · maximize daylight · review meds · limit naps & caffeine · stay calm, avoid confrontation

Care, Drugs & Prevention

  • Goals focus on the caregiver too: reduce caregiver stress; safe environment; dignity; function as long as possible
  • Interventions: regular assessment (incl. family coping) · find cause of behavior change · safety · sleep · caregiver support · teach safety per stage · dietitian, OT, social worker
  • Memory/cognition: cholinesterase inhibitors, NMDA receptor antagonist · also drugs for depression, behavior (antipsychotics, benzos), sleep · ask about medication compliance
  • Brain health: avoid harmful substances · challenge yourself · exercise · stay social · avoid head trauma · treat depression
