How to use this guide

This guide covers nine neuro topics using only the course's 9 slide files. Each condition follows the nursing process: Assess → Plan (goals) → Implement → Evaluate.

  • Slide citations: (S17) = slide 17 of the deck named in that section's heading.
  • [Emphasized] marks points the slides themselves stress: FIRST, LATE, priority, emergency, NEVER, most common. These are the most likely test points.
  • Discuss in class marks a question the slides ask but do not answer. No outside answer is added.
  • Practice questions close each condition. Answers and slide rationales follow in an answer key, so you can quiz yourself first.

Neuro Assessment Toolkit

Assessment tools that appear in the slides: the cranial nerve chart and the scales named in the IICP, Stroke and Dementia decks.

Cranial Nerve Exam (CN chart)

  • IICP: vital sign changes come from pressure on the thalamus, hypothalamus (body temperature), pons and medulla (BP, HR, RR)

I – Olfactory: smell — Test: coffee or soap; patient closes eyes and one nostril, gently inhales; repeat other nostril

II – Optic: central & peripheral vision — Test: central → count fingers held 6 inches in front; peripheral → confrontation test; IICP: blurred vision, diplopia, changes in movements

III – Oculomotor: pupillary constriction — Test: dim lights, bring penlight from periphery, check both pupils constrict; follow finger through 6 cardinal fields of gaze (also CN VI); IICP: ipsilateral dilation of pupil, sluggish or no response to light, inability to move upward, ptosis

IV – Trochlear: moves eye down and toward nose — Test: follow finger as you move it toward the nose

V – Trigeminal: face sensation; temporal & masseter — Test: soft and dull object on forehead, cheek, jaw; open mouth and assess jaw strength

VI – Abducens: eye movement side to side — Test: look toward each ear; 6 cardinal fields; observe for nystagmus or twitching

VII – Facial: facial movement & expression — Test: wrinkle forehead, close eyes, smile, pucker, show teeth, puff cheeks → assess symmetry

VIII – Acoustic: hearing — Test: whispered voice test

IX – Glossopharyngeal: tongue and throat — Test: gag reflex, swallow, taste on back of tongue

X – Vagus: tongue and throat — Test: gag reflex; say "AHHH" → uvula midline, palate rises

XI – Spinal accessory: neck & shoulder movement — Test: shrug shoulders; turn head side to side

XII – Hypoglossal: tongue — Test: stick out tongue → midline

Scales the Slides Use

  • Glasgow Coma Scale: level of consciousness in ↑ICP (IICP S8)
  • Cincinnati scale / FAST: prehospital stroke screen (Stroke S7)
  • NIHSS: primary tool to evaluate and document neuro status in acute stroke; measures severity; predicts short- & long-term outcome; scored initially to compare treatment effect (Stroke S7–8)
  • Confusion Assessment Method: delirium (Dem S3)

Increased Intracranial Pressure (Neuro IICP deck)

Your patient: ICP has risen from a change in brain tissue, blood, or CSF. Under Monro-Kellie, if one increases, another must decrease to maintain ICP (S2)

Assess

  • [Emphasized] Change in LOC: FIRST sign! (Glasgow Coma Scale) (S8)
  • [Emphasized] Change in vital signs (Cushing's triad): LATE sign (oh no!) (S8)
  • ABCs · headache · vomiting · decreased motor function (strength, response, hemiplegia) · posturing: decerebrate, decorticate · abnormal respiratory patterns · ocular signs (dilated, unilateral, fixed) (S8)

[Emphasized] Cushing's triad = Neurologic Emergency!!! (S4)

  1. Systolic hypertension with widening pulse pressure
  2. Bradycardia with bounding pulse
  3. Irregular, decreased respirations

Know the numbers: normal ICP, elevated ICP, CPP — Normal ICP = 5–15 mm Hg · elevated if > 20 mm Hg sustained (S4); CPP = MAP − ICP; cerebral blood flow is the main mechanism for autoregulation (S3); Brain uses about 20% of O₂ and 25% of glucose (S3)

Stages of ↑ICP (4) — 1 Total compensation · 2 ↓ compensation, risk for ↑ICP · 3 Failing compensation, clinical signs (Cushing's triad) · 4 Herniation imminent → death (S4)

Why is this patient at risk? Causes — Affecting blood flow: mass lesion, head injury (surgery), infections, vascular insult, toxic or metabolic encephalopathic conditions (S5); Cerebral edema: vasogenic, cytotoxic, interstitial (S5); Brain injury: primary (at the time: car accident impact, blunt force) vs secondary (hours to days: hypoxia, ischemia, hypotension, edema, ↑ICP) (S6); Head injury terms: skull fracture, concussion (diffuse), diffuse axonal injury, contusion (focal); epidural, subdural, intracerebral hematoma (S7)

Diagnostics to anticipate — CT · MRI · EEG · ICP measurement · cerebral O₂ monitor (LICOX, Neurovent, jugular venous bulb) · Doppler US (transcranial Doppler: noninvasive, monitors cerebrovascular resistance and blood flow) (S11, S16); [Emphasized] No lumbar puncture: contraindicated (S11)

Plan: Goals

  • Maintain a patent airway · ICP within normal limits · normal fluid & electrolyte balance · prevent complications of immobility and decreased LOC (S12)

Implement

Nursing: optimize ICP (S13)

  • [Emphasized] Respiratory function: Priority · maintain ventilator support
  • Keep body temperature 96.8–98.6 °F (36–37 °C)
  • HOB 30° (not higher or lower) · head in neutral alignment · prevent extreme neck flexion · turn slowly
  • Avoid: coughing, straining, Valsalva, hip flexion
  • Pain & anxiety management · fluid & electrolyte balance · minimize abdominal distention (interferes with respiratory effort; may need NG)
  • Quiet, soothing environment · do not do everything at one time
  • Minimize immobility complications · protection from self-injury · psychologic considerations · sedation

ICP monitoring: ventriculostomy (S14)

  • Prevent and monitor for infection · measure as mean pressure · record waveform (normal, elevated, plateau) · evaluate changes with patient condition

Reading looks wrong? Causes of inaccurate readings (5) — CSF leaks · obstruction in catheter / kinks in tubing · differences in height of bolt/transducer · incorrect height of drainage system · bubbles/air in tubing (S14)

Collaborative (S17–18)

  • Treat underlying cause · PaO₂ > 100 mm Hg · PaCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg
  • Nutrition: hypermetabolic & hypercatabolic → ↑ need for glucose · enteral or parenteral · early feeding (within 3 days of injury)
  • Keep normovolemic: IV 0.9% NaCl preferred over D5W or 0.45% NaCl
  • Nursing note: avoiding hypoxia, hypoglycemia, and acidotic state helps control ICP

Mannitol (Osmitrol): what do you monitor? — Plasma expansion, osmotic effect → monitor fluid and electrolyte status (S18)

Hypertonic saline: what do you monitor? — Moves water out of cells into blood → monitor BP and serum sodium (S18)

Corticosteroids: for which edema? — Vasogenic edema (S18)

Drugs for complications / risks of ↑ICP — Antiseizure · antipyretics · sedatives · analgesics · barbiturates (S18)

Evaluate

  • ICP and CPP within normal parameters · no serious increases in ICP during or following care activities · no complications of immobility (S19)

Answer key

  1. C: Change in LOC is the FIRST sign. Change in vital signs (Cushing's triad) is a LATE sign. (IICP S8)
  2. D: Cushing's triad = systolic hypertension with widening pulse pressure, bradycardia with bounding pulse, irregular decreased respirations. (IICP S4)

  4. C: Avoid coughing, straining, Valsalva, and hip flexion. (IICP S13)
  5. B: Diagnostic tests: no lumbar puncture, contraindicated. (IICP S11)
  6. C: IV 0.9% NaCl is preferred over D5W or 0.45% NaCl. (IICP S17)
  7. D: Hypertonic saline moves water out of cells into blood; monitor BP and serum sodium. (Mannitol: monitor fluid and electrolyte status.) (IICP S18)
  8. B: Inaccurate readings come from CSF leaks, obstruction/kinks, differences in height of bolt/transducer, incorrect height of drainage system, bubbles/air in tubing. (IICP S14)

Stroke (Stroke deck)

Your patient: ischemia or hemorrhage into the brain has caused death of brain cells (brain attack, CVA) (S2)

Assess

Who is at risk? [Emphasized: Priority? PREVENTION!!] (S3)

  • Modifiable: hypertension, heart disease, diabetes, smoking, obesity, sleep apnea, metabolic syndrome, lack of exercise, poor diet, drug and alcohol use
  • Non-modifiable: age, gender, ethnicity or race, family history, history of TIA

TIA (S4)

  • [Emphasized] TIA is a warning sign… A STROKE IS COMING!!! · temporary blockage, ischemia without infarction · stroke symptoms typically lasting < 1 hour · 1/3 progress to ischemic stroke

Recognize the stroke (S7)

  • FAST: Face drooping (ask to smile) · Arm weakness (raise both arms, does one drift?) · Speech slurred · Time to call 911
  • Watch for SUDDEN: numbness or weakness (esp. one side) · confusion, trouble speaking or understanding · trouble seeing · trouble walking, dizziness, loss of balance · severe headache with no known cause
  • NIHSS: scored initially; measures severity; compares treatment effect (S7–8)

Which type? Ischemic vs hemorrhagic — Ischemic: most common. Thrombus (60%): associated with HTN and DM, TIA often precedes, recurrence common. Embolism: mostly from heart conditions (afib), could be fat or air (S5); Hemorrhagic: ~13%. Intracerebral: HTN most common cause, occurs during activity, often first a severe headache with N/V. Subarachnoid: often a ruptured cerebral aneurysm; cerebral vasospasm common (ICU 14 days to monitor) (S6)

Symptoms by artery involved — Anterior cerebral: contralateral motor/sensory, sucking or rooting reflex, rigidity, gait problems, loss of proprioception and fine touch; Middle cerebral: dominant side → aphasia, motor and sensory, hemianopsia; non-dominant → neglect, motor and sensory, hemianopsia; Posterior cerebral: hemianopsia, motor, pain, visual hallucinations; Vertebral: cranial nerve, diplopia, dizziness, N/V, dysarthria, dysphasia, coma (S10)

Motor: [Emphasized: most obvious effect] — Impaired mobility, respiratory function, swallowing and speech, gag reflex, self-care. Loss of skilled voluntary movement, akinesia, altered tone and reflexes (hyporeflexia → hyperreflexia); Initial flaccidity (days to several weeks) → then spasticity** (S11)

Communication: aphasia vs dysarthria — Aphasia = stroke damages the dominant hemisphere and affects language. Receptive = loss of comprehension · Expressive = loss of production · Global = total inability · Nonfluent (Broca's) = minimal, slow speech · Fluent (Wernicke's) = speech present, little meaning; Dysarthria = disturbance in muscular control of speech (pronunciation, articulation, phonation) (S12)

Affect, intellect, spatial-perceptual, elimination — Affect: difficulty controlling emotions; exaggerated or unpredictable (S13); Intellect: memory & judgment impaired; Left brain: cautious · Right brain: impulsive (S13); Spatial-perceptual: more likely with right-side stroke: incorrect perception of self and illness, unilateral neglect, agnosia, apraxia (S14); Elimination: problems mostly initial and temporary; one hemisphere affected → prognosis for normal bladder function excellent (S14)

Diagnostics to anticipate — CT · CTA · MRI/MRA · angiography · ultrasound · LP-CSF · EKG · glucose · CBC · PT/PTT (S15)

Plan: Goals (S16)

  • Developed collaboratively with patient, caregiver, family. The patient will: maintain stable or improved LOC · attain maximum physical functioning · maximize self-care · maintain stable body functions · maximize communication · maintain adequate nutrition · avoid complications · maintain effective personal and family coping

Implement

Emergency (ischemic) (S17)

  • [Emphasized] Time of onset is more important patient history · transport to stroke center · meet door-to-needle guidelines
  • ABCs → Stroke Alert → IV/BP management → CT scan to rule out hemorrhage → check blood glucose (treat if hypoglycemic) → other labs → thrombolytic → keep NPO
  • Monitor closely: increasing neuro deficit (NIHSS) · increased ICP (interventions that improve venous drainage) · fluid & electrolytes · glycemic control · Stroke Core Measures
  • Elevated BP is common right after a stroke. Discuss in class: "why?"

BP numbers in the slides; • Ischemic: only reduce if > 220/120 (S17); • Giving fibrinolytics: reduce to < 185/110; monitor closely 24 hrs, SBP < 185 (S17–18); • Hemorrhagic: goal SBP < 160 (S19); • Prevention: SBP < 140 (S26)

tPA: time window, who can't get it, what follows — IV: 3–4.5 hours from onset · intra-arterial: up to 6 hours; Will not give if history of: GI bleed, coagulation disorders, head trauma, major surgery, internal bleeding; Monitor closely 24 hours · aspirin 24–48 hours after · anticoagulants after stabilized · statins · endovascular therapy also an option (S18)

Hemorrhagic (S19)

  • Initial management of hypertension is the main focus (oral and IV agents) → SBP < 160
  • [Emphasized] Anticoagulants and platelet inhibitors are contraindicated
  • Surgery: evacuate hematoma · clipping or coiling of aneurysm · hyperdynamic therapy (hypertension, hypervolemia) · ventriculostomy
  • Calcium channel blockers: treat vasospasm · seizure prophylaxis is situation-specific

Post-acute care by system (S20–25)

  • After stable for 12–24 hours, care shifts from preserving life → lessening disability (S20)

Respiratory: [Emphasized: nursing priority] — Frequent assessment · O₂ · oral care · suction · prevent aspiration: swallow eval to rule out dysphagia** (S21)

Neurologic — Continued assessments: NIHSS, LOC, ICP (S21)

Cardiovascular — Fluid homeostasis (dehydration vs overload) · vital signs · VTE prophylaxis: keep patient moving, ROM, SCD/TED hose, low molecular weight heparin (S21)

Musculoskeletal: prevent contractures — ROM · trochanter roll at hip (prevents external rotation) · hand cones · posterior leg splints, footboards, high-top tennis shoes (prevent foot drop) · arm supports with slings and lap boards · avoid pulling the patient by the arm (shoulder displacement) (S21)

Skin — Position changes, special mattresses, wheelchair cushions · position on the weak or paralyzed side for only 30 minutes · skin hygiene · emollients · early mobility (S22)

Nutrition — Quick assessment · IV fluids initially, may need enteral · NPO until swallow evaluation · physical supports for self-feeding (S22)

Bowel & bladder — [Emphasized] Constipation = most common bowel problem → stool softeners or fiber, physical activity, bowel retraining · bladder: incontinence in acute stage → bladder retraining · avoid indwelling catheters (S22)

Talking with a patient who has aphasia — [Emphasized] most difficult problem for patient and family · simple, short sentences · assess for understanding · yes/no questions · normal volume/tone · give time for response · decrease stimuli · use gestures (S23)

Neglect & homonymous hemianopsia — Break tasks into simple steps · environmental controls · nonslip socks · educate about one-side neglect (may forget to shave half of face) · arrange environment with things in the visual field, including food → then teach to scan (S23)

Coping — Atypical emotional responses: distract · explain to patient and family that outbursts may occur · calm environment · avoid shaming or scolding · explain what happened, diagnosis, procedures · active listening · include family in goal planning · family conferences · social services referral (S25)

Teaching: prevention (S26)

  • Decreased sodium · normal body weight · low saturated fat · limit alcohol · SBP < 140 · exercise 150 min/week · no smoking · control diabetes · take cardiac medications · left atrial appendage occlusion · antiplatelets · anticoagulants for Afib

Evaluate

  • The slides list goals (Plan) but no separate stroke evaluation slide. Use the goals above as your outcomes (S16)

Meningitis & Encephalitis (Meningitis/Encephalitis deck)

Your patient: acute inflammation of the meningeal tissue around the brain and spinal cord (meningitis) or of the brain itself (encephalitis) (S2, S9)

Assess

  • [Emphasized] Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency; untreated mortality near 100% (S2)
  • Risk: students in dorms and prisoners · usually fall, winter, early spring (S2)

Key signs of bacterial meningitis — Fever · severe headache · nausea, vomiting · nuchal rigidity · skin rash and petechiae (if meningococcus) · coma (5–10%, poor prognosis) (S3)

Other signs — Photophobia · ↓LOC · signs of ↑ICP · seizures in 1/3 of cases · headache becomes progressively worse with vomiting and irritability (S3)

Encephalitis: what you'll see — Nonspecific onset: fever, headache, N/V · signs appear in 2–3 days · from minimal mental status change to coma · any CNS abnormality can occur (S10); Caused by viruses; ticks or mosquitoes can transmit; CMV encephalitis common with AIDS (S9)

Plan: Goals (S4)

  • Return to maximal neurologic functioning · minimize complications · resolve the infection · control pain and discomfort

Implement

Acute (S5)

  • Obtain cultures (nasopharyngeal mucosa and CSF) BEFORE starting antibiotics · lumbar puncture to check CSF is normally performed
  • Start empiric therapy, discontinue if viral · [Emphasized] early antibiotic treatment is critical
  • Respiratory isolation until cultures are negative · treat close contacts with prophylactic antibiotics · close observation and frequent assessment
  • Comfort: relief for head and neck pain · position for comfort · darkened room and cool cloth over the eyes (photophobia) · minimize stimuli · avoid restraints · provide safety · family at bedside for calming effect

Prevention & home care (S6)

  • Immunize high-risk populations: recommended at age 11; if not done, college freshman (dorm life)
  • Several weeks of convalescence · increase activity as tolerated · nutrition · rest and sleep · progressive ROM and warm baths for muscle rigidity · ongoing assessment of vision, hearing, cognition, motor and sensory · support family and caregivers

Viral meningitis & encephalitis

  • Viral meningitis: "It's a virus… manage symptoms" (S8)
  • Encephalitis: symptomatic and supportive (incl. West Nile) · intensive care may be required initially · antiseizure drugs, may be prophylactic · prevention: environmental mosquito control, repellents (S11)

Evaluate (S7)

  • Demonstrates appropriate cognitive function · oriented to person, place, time · body temperature within normal range · reports satisfaction with pain control

Seizures (Seizures deck)

Your patient: sudden, abnormal, excessive electrical discharge of neurons in the brain (S2). Seizure disorder/epilepsy = 2 or more seizures more than 24 hours apart without an underlying problem (S3)

Assess

  • Causes: stroke, TBI, hyper/hypoglycemia, electrolyte imbalance, alcohol withdrawal, high fever in children. If it stops when the systemic/metabolic problem is corrected, it is not a seizure disorder (S3)

Phases (4) — Prodromal: precedes the seizure with signs or activity · Aura: sensory warning, part of the seizure · Ictal: beginning of symptom to end of seizure activity · Postictal: rest and recovery (S4)

Generalized: both hemispheres — Impaired consciousness (seconds to minutes) · Motor: tonic-clonic, tonic, clonic, atonic · Non-motor (absence): typical, atypical (S5)

Focal: one hemisphere — Focal aware: conscious and alert; unusual feelings (sudden sob, anger, nausea); twitching; sees, tastes, or feels things that are not real; Focal impaired awareness: loss of consciousness (change in awareness), eyes open, may do embarrassing things, unaware; lasts 1–2 minutes, then resumes usual activity unaware; Motor terms: atonic (loss of tone) · tonic (sustained stiffening) · clonic (rhythmic jerking) · myoclonic (very rapid, irregular, brief jerking) · automatisms (lip smacking, repetitive behaviors) · non-motor: emotional or autonomic (S6)

Status epilepticus & subclinical seizure — Status epilepticus: continuous seizure activity; seizures in rapid succession with no return of consciousness; Subclinical: having seizures, but sedatives used so you cannot tell (S7)

After the seizure: what you assess — Bitten tongue, soft tissue damage, cyanosis · abnormal respiratory rate · apnea (ictal) · absent/abnormal breath sounds · airway occlusion · hypertension, tachy/bradycardia · bowel/urinary incontinence, excessive salivation · weakness, paralysis, ataxia (postictal) · abnormal CT, MRI, EEG; History: anything different? missed dose? took dose late? any injury? other sickness (vomiting)? (S9)

Diagnostics: rule out an underlying cause — CBC · CMP · CT, MRI, PET · LP (CSF) · UA · liver enzymes · EEG (S10)

Plan: Goals (S8)

  • Free from injury during seizure · optimal mental and physical functioning while taking antiseizure medications · satisfactory psychosocial functioning

Implement

Tonic-clonic: emergency care (S11)

  1. Ensure airway
  2. [Emphasized] Protect from injury (NEVER restrain): pad rails · help to floor if in chair · protect head · loosen constrictive clothing
  3. Do not put anything in mouth · turn patient on side
  4. Establish IV (or IO) · stay with patient, time seizure
  5. Give medications to stop seizure (benzodiazepines)
  6. Prepare to suction and assist ventilations after seizure; intubate if necessary
  • Most seizures are self-limiting and do not cause injury · first-time seizure or injury → seek medical care · main treatment = medications · potentially surgery, VNS, responsive neurostimulation, ketogenic diet (S11)

Medications (S13)

For Drugs
Tonic-clonic and focal onset Phenytoin, Tegretol, Depakote
Generalized nonmotor and myoclonic Zarontin, divalproex, Klonopin
Focal Lamictal, Keppra
Status epilepticus Short-acting benzodiazepines (IM, buccal, intranasal, IV); IV Ativan preferred if IV available → followed by long acting
  • Assess for common side effects and signs of toxicity · teach medication compliance · effects differ in older adults (S13)

Teaching (S12)

  • [Emphasized] Prevention of recurring seizures is the major goal → adherence; do not adjust dose without physician; keep regular appointments
  • Safety measures · helmet if risk of head injury · general health habits · identify and avoid precipitating events · avoid excessive alcohol, fatigue, loss of sleep
  • Teach family emergency management · emotional support and coping · employment discrimination · restricted driving · medical alert bracelets · referrals

Complications (S14)

  • Severe injury and death from trauma; [Emphasized] patients who lose consciousness are at greatest risk · mortality 2–3× general population · [Emphasized] effect on lifestyle = most common complication (stigma, employment and education limits, driving sanctions)

Evaluate (S15)

  • Breathing pattern adequate to meet O₂ needs · no seizure-related injury · accepts seizure disorder and maintains compliant behavior · acknowledges seizure occurred · therapeutic drug levels

Headaches (Headaches deck)

Your patient: probably the most common pain humans experience. Most are primary (migraine, tension-type, cluster); secondary headaches come from another condition (sinus infection, neck injury, stroke) (S2)

Assess: which headache is this?

Tension-type — Bilateral · pressing/tightening · constant dull pressure · frontal-occipital · mild or moderate · associated with neck pain and ↑ tone in cervical/neck muscles · may occur intermittently for weeks, months, years · photophobia, phonophobia · no nausea/vomiting · no prodrome (S4)

Migraine — Unilateral (primarily) or bilateral throbbing · triggering event or factor (certain food, trauma, menstruation) · genetic link (50%); Without aura = most common · with aura = only 25%; aura 10–30 min before (visual, sensory, motor); Photophobia · steady throbbing 4–72 hours · patient "hibernates" (seeks shelter from noise, light, odors, people) · can have N/V · can have numbness/tingling of face (S5)

Cluster — Rare · sharp, stabbing · repeatedly (daily) for weeks to months, then remission · same time of day · one of the most severe · trigeminal nerve implicated · alcohol dietary trigger; Unilateral, minutes to 3 hours, around the eye radiating to temple, forehead, cheek, nose, gums · edema around eye, tearing, ptosis · facial flushing or pallor · rhinitis · constricted pupil · patient agitated and restless (S6)

Secondary causes to rule out — Subarachnoid hemorrhage · brain tumors · intracranial masses · vascular abnormalities · trigeminal neuralgia · eyes, nose, teeth disease · systemic illness (bacteremia, CO poisoning, mountain sickness, polycythemia vera) · medication overuse headache (acetaminophen, NSAIDs, aspirin, sumatriptan, opioids) (S3)

Diagnostics — Neurological exam · CT if concern for secondary cause · CBC, BMP, UA (S7)

Plan

  • If no systemic disease is the cause, the type of headache guides therapy; symptomatic and prophylactic therapies are used (S8)

Implement

Nursing & teaching (S8)

  • Headache diary · daily exercise, relaxation, socializing · alternatives: relaxation, meditation, yoga, self-hypnosis
  • Quiet, dim environment · massage and heat packs for tension-type
  • Written note of medications to prevent accidental overdose
  • Teach prophylactic treatment · dietary counseling for food triggers · avoid smoking and environmental triggers (foods, smoking, strong perfumes; may change over time)

Drugs by type (S9)

Tension — Non-opioid analgesic: aspirin/NSAID · caffeine, sedative, muscle relaxant · preventive: tricyclic antidepressant, antiseizure, muscle relaxant

Migraine — Mild–moderate: NSAID, aspirin, caffeine analgesics · [Emphasized] moderate–severe: triptans first line · triptans contraindicated in some patients: cause vasoconstriction · preventive treatment

Cluster — Triptans standard · high-flow 100% oxygen at 7–12 L/min for 20 min · preventive

Evaluate (S10)

  • Reports satisfaction with pain relief · uses drug and nondrug measures appropriately

Parkinson's Disease (Parkinson's deck)

Your patient: disease of the basal ganglia; chronic, progressive, neurodegenerative, no acute exacerbations. Lack of dopamine disrupts the dopamine–acetylcholine balance (S2–3)

Assess

Classic triad (+ postural instability) — Tremor: "pill rolling" · Rigidity: "cogwheel" · Bradykinesia: slowing in initiating and executing movement, loss of automatic movement · also postural instability (unable to stop) (S4)

Progression — Gradual onset · early: mild tremor, slight limp, ↓ arm swing · later: shuffling, propulsive gait, arms flexed, loss of postural reflexes · speech changes, communication, sleep, eating concerns (S4)

Non-motor symptoms — Depression, anxiety, apathy, fatigue, pain, incontinence, constipation, erectile dysfunction, memory changes (S4)

  • Increases with age · more common in men · Lewy bodies · cause unknown (S3)

Plan: Goals (S5)

  • Maximize neurologic function · maintain independence in ADLs as long as possible · prevent injury · optimize psychosocial well-being

Implement

Nursing (S6)

  • Maintenance of good health · encourage independence · avoid contractures and falls · promote exercise
  • Diet: well-balanced · food cut into small pieces · 6 small meals · moderate fiber · limit protein to evening
  • Bradykinesia help: rock side to side to start · think about stepping over a line · lift toes when stepping · chairs with arms (back legs on small blocks) · remove rugs and excess furniture · simplify clothing (no buttons, hooks) · elevated toilet seats · ottoman for legs · sleep hygiene
  • Help adjust lifestyle · caregivers may experience stress with progression (e.g., dementia)

Collaborative (S7)

  • [Emphasized] Levodopa with Carbidopa (Sinemet): primary treatment · one drug best, may need combination · excessive dopaminergic drugs → paradoxic intoxication
  • No cure; relieve symptoms · nutrition, physical, speech therapy · surgery: DBS, ablation

Evaluate

  • Exercises to deter atrophy and contractures · uses assistive devices appropriately · nutrition adequate · safe passage of fluids/solids from mouth to stomach · communication meets needs (S9)
  • Watch for complications (↑ with progression): dysphagia → malnutrition and aspiration · weakness, immobility · orthostatic hypotension · falls · dyskinesias · neuropsychological problems · [Emphasized] dementia in 70% · pneumonia, UTI, skin breakdown (S8)

Multiple Sclerosis (MS/MG deck)

Your patient: chronic, progressive, degenerative CNS disorder: segmental demyelination of nerve fibers of brain and spinal cord (S2). Onset 20–40 yrs; women 2–3× more; more in temperate climates (S3)

Assess

  • [Emphasized] Vision changes often first sign (S4)
  • Muscle weakness or fatigue · tingling or numbness · muscle spasms · Lhermitte's sign (electric shock down the spine) · pain · altered bowel and bladder · cognitive and emotional changes (S4)

Pathology (3) & disease patterns (4) — Chronic inflammation · demyelination · gliosis (scarring); Relapsing-remitting (85%) · primary-progressive · secondary-progressive · progressive-relapsing (S3)

Plan: Goals (S5)

  • Maximize neuromuscular function · independence in ADLs · manage disabling fatigue · optimize psychosocial well-being · adjust to illness · ↓ factors that precipitate exacerbations

Implement

Nursing (S6)

  • Identify and avoid triggers: infection, trauma, live virus immunizations, childbirth, stress, climate
  • Reassure during diagnostic phase · help with anxiety and grief · during acute exacerbation prevent complications of immobility
  • Balance exercise and rest · minimize caffeine · nutritious meals · high-fiber diet for constipation · medication management · self-catheterization if necessary · emotional adjustment, lifestyle changes

Collaborative (S7)

  • No cure · disease-modifying: interferons, monoclonal antibodies, synthetic agents, [Emphasized] immunomodulators (often used first) · corticosteroids
  • Symptom drugs: spasticity, fatigue, pain (antidepressants, antiseizure), depression, bowel/bladder (anticholinergics), sexual problems, cognitive changes
  • PT: decrease spasticity · increase coordination · train unaffected muscles · water exercise · surgery

Evaluate (S8)

  • Maintains or improves muscle strength and mobility · assistive devices used appropriately · maintains urinary continence · makes decisions about lifestyle modifications

Your patient: autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction; fluctuating weakness of skeletal muscle; may respond to removal of the thymus (S9)
